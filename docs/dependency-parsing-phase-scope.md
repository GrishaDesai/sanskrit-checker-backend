# Sanskrit Proof Checker — dependency-parsing phase: UDPipe 1 evaluated and declined

**Status: evaluation complete, layer NOT built.** Original run 2026-09-06.
**Rebuilt and independently re-measured from scratch 2026-09-09.**

This phase was scoped to answer one question: can a dependency parser solve the
**two named jobs** left over from the Vidyut phase —

- **(a) word-order-independent subject identification** — the real subject
  follows the verb, which the engine's pre-verb scan cannot reach
  (e.g. `वनं गच्छति रामः`), and
- **(b) asyndetic coordination detection** — coordinated nominals of differing
  gender being flagged as an agreement mismatch (e.g. `तस्य दण्डः शुल्कं च भवति`),

— well enough to gate a 🟡-tier check without dragging the gold set's
false-positive avoidance down.

**The answer is no, and the evidence is not marginal.** No configuration
approaches a usable precision bar. This phase added **no code to `app/`**.

The most consequential finding is not the parser's accuracy but §5: the engine
cannot tell the parser where the verbs are, which **reverses the sequencing**
the Vidyut phase assumed.

## 2. The treebank, reported honestly before training

| | **UD_Sanskrit-UFAL** | UD_Sanskrit-Vedic |
|---|---|---|
| sentences | **230** | 21,477 |
| syntactic words | **1,843** | 161,959 |
| train/dev/test split | **none — a test file only** | yes |
| script | Devanagari | IAST (transliterated here) |
| genre | Pañcatantra fiction | Vedic prose/verse |
| `nsubj` / `nsubj:pass` | 168 / 36 | 14,532 / 0 |
| `conj` / `cc` | 114 / 34 | 10,827 / 3,302 |

`sa_ufal-ud-test.conllu` **is** the treebank: UD ships it as a test-only,
low-resource treebank. Mean sentence length 8.0 tokens. For scale, 1,843
tokens is roughly **1%** of a normal UD training treebank.

The two target phenomena **are** present, which is why the experiment was worth
running:

- **46 post-verbal subjects** (22.5% of the 204 subjects with a real head)
- **84 nominal coordination pairs, 17 of them differing in gender** — including
  `भिक्षया`(Fem) + `कृषिकर्मणा`(Neut) + `व्यवहारेण`(Masc), structurally
  identical to our own `दण्डः`/`शुल्कं` false positive

That is the entire supervision signal for both jobs: **46 examples and 17
examples.**

## 3. Baseline parser accuracy

Because 230 sentences makes a single held-out split noise, the headline is
**10-fold cross-validation over contiguous blocks** (contiguous, not shuffled,
so adjacent sentences of one fable cannot leak across the split), scored with
**gold tokenisation and gold tags** — the optimistic setting, since Vidyut
supplies the morphology and UDPipe only has to parse.

| config | training data | UAS | LAS |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C1** — defaults, raw UFAL | 1,843 tok | **60.55%** | **49.00%** |
| C2 — lemma embeddings, mincount=1 | 1,843 tok | 57.62% | 45.79% |
| C4 — defaults, surface-projected UFAL | 1,474 tok | 61.13% | 48.30% |
| C3b — 5,000 Vedic sentences → UFAL | 41,270 tok | 52.31% | 31.69% |

Per-fold UAS on C1 ranges **37.0–79.5**, which is what a 23-sentence test fold
buys.

**C2 was measured and rejected** in the original run and was not re-run in the
rebuild. It was motivated by two real observations in the trainer's own log —
form embeddings cover only 51.2% of tokens, and lemma embeddings are off by
default, both bad for a heavily inflected language. It nonetheless made
everything that matters worse: coordination precision fell 52.3% → 12.1% and
subject precision 34.4% → 27.3%.

Relation-level detail for C1 shows where the trouble is:

```
root        gold 230  R 71.3%     nsubj      gold 168  R 48.2%   pred 239
nmod        gold 147  R 72.8%     conj       gold 114  R  7.9%   pred  55
obj         gold 101  R 51.5%     nsubj:pass gold  36  R  5.6%   pred   2
                                  nsubj:cop  gold  19  R  0.0%   pred   0
```

`nsubj` is **over-predicted by 71** (239 predicted against 168 gold), and
`conj` — job (b)'s entire mechanism — is at **7.9% recall**.

## 4. The acceptance test, on the two named jobs

Aggregate LAS is the wrong yardstick here, in both directions, so each job was
scored on its own terms:

- **Job (a)** does not need a full parse, only "which nominal is this verb's
  subject" — and only where the subject *follows* the verb. Reported alongside
  `nsubj` **precision**, because a check gated on a hallucinated subject is a
  brand-new false positive.
- **Job (b)** does not need `conj`'s exact head, only "are these two nominals in
  one coordination" — scored as membership in the transitive closure over
  `conj` arcs. **Precision is the number that matters**: this gate *suppresses*
  a gender flag, so a false coordination silently hides a real error, while a
  missed one merely leaves today's false positive in place.

### 4.1 All four configurations, gold tags — the optimistic upper bound

| config | post-verbal subj | **subj precision** | **coord precision** | coord recall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 — raw UFAL | 5.4% (3/56) | **34.4%** | **52.3%** | 9.2% |
| C2 — lemma embeddings | 3.6% (2/56) | 27.3% | 12.1% | 1.1% |
| C4 — surface-projected | 12.0% (6/50) | 32.8% | 17.5% | 3.8% |
| C3b — 5k Vedic transfer | **16.1%** (9/56) | 26.8% | 48.1% | **10.6%** |
| **best-of-any-config ceiling** | **16.1%** | **34.4%** | **52.3%** | **10.6%** |

The bottom row is the fair statement of the ceiling: it takes the best value of
each metric from whichever configuration achieved it, so **no single model is
this good**. Even so:

**Job (a) tops out at 16.1% where it is needed, with subject precision never
above 34.4%.** The parser's subject predictions are wrong roughly **twice as
often as they are right** in every configuration. Gating subject–verb agreement
on this would manufacture about two new false subjects for every one it fixed.

**Job (b) tops out at 52.3% precision.** A suppression gate that is wrong about
half the time does not suppress false positives; it hides real errors at a
comparable rate — a worse failure than the one it was meant to fix.

Note the trade-off across rows: the configurations that find more post-verbal
subjects (C4, C3b) do so by predicting *more* subjects, and their precision
falls accordingly. **Recall on this job is bought with precision, and precision
is the constraint.**

### 4.2 On the actual false positives, through the deployment path

The probe sentences are the real ones — the five remaining DCS syntax false
positives verbatim, plus two canonical examples from the phase brief — run
through engine tokenisation → Vidyut morphology → parser.

| # | sentence | job | C1 | C4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | वनं गच्छति रामः | a | ❌ | ❌ |
| 2 | उन्मत्तो मूढ इत्येवं मन्यन्ते इतरे जनाः | a | ❌ | ❌ |
| 3 | स्नातानुलिप्तं यं चापि भजन्ते नीलमक्षिकाः | a | ❌ | ❌ |
| 4 | पुस्तकं पठति बालकः *(constructed)* | a | ❌ | ✅ |
| 5 | तस्य दण्डः शुल्कं च भवति | b | ❌ | ❌ |
| 6 | …चतुष्पञ्चाशत्पणो दण्डः शुल्कं मातुर् भोगः… | b | ❌ | ❌ |
| 7 | कक्षौ स्तनौ गलः पृष्ठं जघनम् ऊरू च स्थानानि | b | ❌ | ❌ |

**C1: job (a) 0/4, job (b) 0/3. C4: job (a) 1/4, job (b) 0/3.**

The single pass is a three-word sentence written for this test. **None of the
five real DCS false positives resolves under any model.**

An eighth probe — `अस्ति दाक्षिणात्ये जनपदे महिलारोप्यं नाम नगरम्` — is excluded
from the score on discovery that it **is a training sentence** (UFAL line 172).
It passed on the first run and would have been the only pass; that is
memorisation, not generalisation. The probe script now detects this
automatically and discounts any probe found in the training file.

Case 7 is the instructive near-miss: the parser *did* build most of the
enumeration, but `पृष्ठं` — one of the exact two words in our false positive —
landed outside the coordination as `nmod`. Getting an enumeration 80% right
does not help a gate asked about one specific pair.

## 5. Why it fails — and one thing that turned out not to be the reason

### 5.1 Tokenisation — a mismatch that turned out to be fixable

UD_Sanskrit-UFAL is annotated over **syntactic words**: compounds split into
members, sandhi resolved, the surface string retained only as a multiword-token
range. Our engine works over **surface tokens**.

Measured raw, this looks fatal — and it was originally written up as a
structural blocker before the claim was properly tested. **That was wrong.** Two
things had been conflated: a genuine annotation-granularity difference, and
punctuation, which the engine's `WORD_PATTERN` excludes by design and which
accounts for 195 of the treebank's 1,843 tokens.

Collapsing each multiword range onto the single surface node the engine would
actually emit (`scripts/dep/collapse_mwt.py`) and discounting punctuation:

| | raw treebank | surface-projected |
|---|---|---|
| sentences with a multiword range | 135 / 230 (58.7%) | 0 |
| engine tokenisation **matches** gold | **97 / 230 (42.2%)** | **225 / 230 (97.8%)** |
| engine tokenisation differs | 132 (57.4%) | 4 (1.7%) |
| tokens: engine vs treebank | 1,272 vs 1,630 (**1.28**) | 1,272 vs 1,268 (**1.00**) |

> **Correction made during the 2026-09-09 rebuild.** The previous version of
> this table reported the raw-treebank match rate as **33 / 230 (14.3%)**.
> Re-measurement gives **97 / 230 (42.2%)**. The cause is that
> `tokenisation_gap.py` discounts `PUNCT` unconditionally (line 29), so 14.3%
> came from an earlier version of the script that did not — meaning the table
> compared an *uncorrected* raw figure against a *corrected* surface figure and
> overstated the gap being closed. The honest like-for-like improvement is
> **42.2% → 97.8%**, not 14.3% → 97.8%. Token counts and the entire surface
> column reproduced exactly. **No conclusion changes**: the projection still
> removes essentially the whole tokenisation gap.

**Tokenisation is not the blocker.** It is cheaply and near-losslessly fixable,
and the projection preserves the target signal — 45 post-verbal subjects (23.8%)
and 15 differing-gender nominal coordinations survive the collapse. C4 trains on
the projected treebank accordingly, and it did **not** rescue the acceptance
test: post-verbal subjects improved 5.4% → 12.0% while subject precision stayed
flat at ~33% and coordination precision *fell* 52.3% → 17.5%.

### 5.2 The morphology the parser would actually receive is not gold

The cross-validation gives the parser gold tags. In the product the tags come
from `vidyut.kosha`. With the §5.1 projection the comparison covers **225 of 230
sentences, 1,254 tokens**:

| | |
|---|---|
| UPOS agreement with UD gold | 34.3% |
| UPOS agreement, **coarse** (nominal / verbal / pronominal / indeclinable) | **48.9%** |
| Case — recall / precision | 27.7% / 69.4% |
| Gender — recall / precision | 16.5% / 59.0% |
| Number — recall / precision | 63.7% / 80.9% |
| tokens reaching the parser with **no** morphology at all | **39.0%** |
| tokens the engine does not recognise at all (`X`) | 23.3% |

The coarse row is the fair one: the bridge can only emit `NOUN/VERB/PRON/ADV/X`,
so a strict comparison penalises it for gold distinctions it never had the
vocabulary to make. Even scored fairly it agrees on **about half** of tokens.

Note the asymmetry: where Vidyut *does* commit to a case it is right 69.4% of
the time, but it declines to commit on nearly three-quarters of tokens, because
the Kosha returns disagreeing readings and one FEATS column cannot hold a
disjunction. Underspecifying is the honest choice — the alternative, emitting a
top-ranked guess, is a nominative-biased bug — but it means the parser works
nearly blind.

**A note on how §5.2 was obtained**, because the first attempt was wrong in a
way worth recording. The bridge originally ranked ambiguous readings
Prathamā-first and emitted the top one, which stamped `Case=Nom` on every
ambiguous token (the locative `जनपदे` came out nominative) and let a तिङन्त
homograph outrank an ordinary noun (`रामः` was handed over as `VERB Person=1`).
Both were defects in the *measuring apparatus*. The bridge now emits a feature
only where every surviving reading agrees, and applies the engine's own
पुरुष-consistency gate (अस्मद्युत्तमः १.४.१०७ / युष्मद्युपपदे … मध्यमः १.४.१०५).
Fixing it made the probe results **worse**, not better.

### 5.3 The finding that matters most: the parser cannot see the verbs

The coarse disagreements are not spread evenly. They concentrate:

| gold → bridge | count |
|---|---|
| **VERB → NOUN** | **124** |
| **VERB → X** | **109** |
| NOUN → X | 96 |
| ADV → NOUN | 87 |
| PRON → NOUN | 77 |
| DET → NOUN | 30 |

**233 of 1,254 compared tokens are verbs that reach the parser tagged as nouns
or as unknown words.** This is exactly the limit the Vidyut phase recorded:
`verb_readings` indexes **लट्-कर्तरि only**, so उच्यते, चकार and स्यात् are not
verbs as far as the pipeline is concerned. The `ADV → NOUN` and `PRON → NOUN`
rows are the same homograph collisions that phase found; `NOUN → X` is its
compound/sandhi ceiling.

A dependency parser's central job here is attaching a subject to **its verb**.
It cannot do that on input where the verbs are labelled nouns.

**This reverses the sequencing the Vidyut phase assumed.** That document shelved
the completed lakāra extension with the note that it "becomes valuable after a
dependency parser lands, not before". The evidence here says the opposite: **full
lakāra coverage is a prerequisite for a parser being able to work at all**. The
extension exists and was measured (11 lakāras × कर्तरि/कर्मणि, 412,609 surface
forms); its recorded cost was a false-positive regression *in the rule-based
syntax layer*, which is a **separate consumer** from the parser's tagger input —
those two effects must be measured separately.

## 6. The Vedic option — the one remaining lever

UD_Sanskrit-Vedic is 88× larger than UFAL, so "not enough data" had to be tested
rather than asserted. It is IAST, transliterated to Devanagari offline with
`vidyut.lipi` (no new dependency). The two corpora are disjoint, so a model
trained on Vedic can be scored on the **whole** of UFAL, making C3b directly
comparable to C1.

**C3b: 5,000 Vedic sentences (41,270 tokens, ~22× UFAL), 1,196 s to train.**

| | C1 (1,843 tok, in-domain) | C3b (41,270 tok, out-of-domain) |
|---|---|---|
| UAS / LAS | 60.55 / 49.00 | 52.31 / 31.69 |
| post-verbal subjects | 5.4% | **16.1%** |
| subject precision | **34.4%** | 26.8% |
| coordination precision | **52.3%** | 48.1% |
| coordination recall | 9.2% | **10.6%** |

22× the data moves post-verbal subject recall from 5.4% to 16.1% — the best
figure in this phase — and moves **subject precision the wrong way**, 34.4% →
26.8%. Coordination is flat. Overall LAS drops sharply because Vedic is a
different language stage with a different annotation style (`nsubj:pass` 0,
`nmod:poss` 0, no `PUNCT` at all — hence `punct` recall 0.0%).

**More data does not buy precision, which is the constraint.**

A full-corpus run over all 21,477 Vedic sentences remains **not run**. The 5k
result already answers the question the lever was pulled for, and §4.1 shows the
trade-off runs the wrong way: more data bought recall and cost precision.

## 7. Steps deliberately not taken

Building the two checks, and re-running the full eval with the layer included,
were **not executed, by the phase's own acceptance criteria**.

The standing rule is that a change is kept only if it clearly improves the
numbers without new regressions, and that a 🟡-tier addition must reduce net
error. A subject gate at ≤34.4% precision and a coordination gate at ≤52.3%
precision cannot do that; they would add false positives to a system whose
central promise is that its strongest claims are never wrong. Shipping them
behind a 🟡 label would not rescue them — 🟡 means "a statistical prediction
worth your attention", not "a coin flip".

**Vidyut phase integrity, verified 2026-09-09.** This phase adds no code to
`app/`. The gold suite was run before and after the full delete-and-rebuild and
the output was **byte-identical**:

```
in-scope pass rate     139/155  (89.7%)
expected=error          44/60   (73.3%)
expected=correct        56/56  (100.0%)   <- false-positive avoidance intact
expected=review         39/39  (100.0%)
```

## 8. What would actually have to change, in order

1. **Full lakāra coverage in `verb_readings` (§5.3).** 233 of 1,254 compared
   tokens are verbs the pipeline cannot see. Hard prerequisite for anything
   reasoning about verb arguments, parser or not. Measure its effect on the
   rule-based layer separately from its effect as parser input — different
   consumers, different risk profiles.
2. **Compound segmentation (§5.2).** 23.3% of tokens reach the parser as `X` and
   39.0% carry no morphology. The same lever the Vidyut phase named as largest,
   now with a second beneficiary.
3. **Data volume.** 1,843 in-domain tokens cannot train a parser, and 46
   post-verbal subjects cannot teach post-verbal subjects. Not a hyperparameter
   problem — C2 confirmed that by making things worse — and §6 shows
   out-of-domain volume does not substitute.
4. **Only then, a parser.** UDPipe 1 remains the right tool to retry with, and
   the surface-projection pipeline (§5.1) is built and working for that day.

**What is now known that was not before:** the two named jobs are not blocked by
the absence of a parser. They are blocked upstream — by verb invisibility and by
the compound-segmentation ceiling — which means a parser could not have
succeeded here regardless of which one was chosen. That is a stronger and more
useful result than "UDPipe 1 scored poorly".
